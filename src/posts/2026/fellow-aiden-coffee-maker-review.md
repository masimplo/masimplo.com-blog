I did the research before buying this one — read the reviews, watched the teardown videos, compared it against the usual pour-over ritual I'd been doing by hand for years. The Fellow Aiden gets called the smartest drip coffee maker money can buy, and for the first couple of weeks I would not have argued with that. Then the novelty did what novelty always does, and I was left with an honest question: is the coffee actually better, or was I just having fun.

## The brew profiles are the actual product

Most drip machines push water using thermal-siphon pressure — heat the water, let the resulting pressure force it up a tube — which is why their flow rate and temperature drift over the course of a brew. The Aiden is one of the only ones on the market that skips that entirely and uses a water pump to inject specific volumes at specific times, decoupled from whatever the heating element happens to be doing. That's the actual engineering behind the SCA-grade temperature control and the bypass valve, and it's the reason the brew profiles can be precise instead of just labeled that way. On top of that mechanism sits the thing that makes it feel like 2026 rather than 1995: the app. Fellow ships AI-tuned brew profiles for specific coffees — down to the gram, the basket, the bloom time — and you can dial in your own for whatever bag you're currently working through. The first few weeks of owning this thing is less "making coffee" and more "tuning a config file you get to drink afterward." I loved that part unreasonably.

## Cold brew in a couple of hours instead of a couple of days

The feature that impressed me most wasn't even the hot coffee. Traditional cold brew is a waiting game — you set it up, forget about it, and come back the better part of a day later. The Aiden's cold brew profile does a hot bloom followed by a slow cold drip and gets you good, high-extraction cold brew in a couple of hours, not a couple of days. The first time I timed it against my usual overnight steep and got a comparable cup in a fraction of the time, that's when the machine justified its own existence to me, price aside.

## Once it's dialed in, it becomes furniture

This machine goes through the exact same arc as a new tool in my terminal: intense tuning for the first couple of weeks, a burst of "look what I can configure now," and then, once you've got two or three coffee origins dialed in, you stop opening the app at all. You just tap the profile and walk away. That's a genuinely good outcome — *set and forget* is what you actually want from a coffee maker at 6:30am — but it also means the most exciting phase of ownership is also the shortest one. Unlike a dev tool you get bored of, though, you can't just uninstall this and move on for free; it's sitting on your counter having cost you real money.

## The coffee is good. Not 10x-the-price good.

This is the honest verdict. The coffee this thing makes is good — consistent, well-extracted, noticeably better than a basic drip machine. It is not ten times better than a well-executed manual pour-over, and the Aiden costs a lot more than ten times a basic dripper. You are paying for repeatability and for not having to think about it every morning, not for a categorically different cup. If your baseline was already careful manual brewing, the delta is real but modest. If your baseline was a gas-station drip machine, the delta is enormous and the price starts looking a lot more reasonable.

## Would I buy it again

Probably not, and that surprised me to write down. I loved the experience — the dialing in, the app, the cold brew party trick, the two weeks of feeling like my coffee routine had been upgraded. But the honest accounting is that the jump from "good manual coffee" to "good machine coffee" was smaller than the price tag implied, and the part I use daily now — tap a saved profile, walk away — is a feature I'd happily pay a fraction of the price for on a much simpler machine.

The experience was worth having once. Whether it's worth owning twice is a different question, and I landed on no.
