The shot starts the way it always has. The pump hums, the needle climbs, and then — a few seconds in — it stops climbing and starts to sag. The espresso keeps flowing. It just flows without conviction, like the machine has lost interest halfway through.

I have owned my Lelit Anita (the PL042TEMD) for about five years. It has been serviced by me, fed very good beans, and treated with the kind of attention some people reserve for their cars. This was the first time it let me down — and the Lelit Anita pump replacement that followed turned into the best upgrade the machine has had. It failed the way pumps usually do: slowly enough that I noticed late.

## The slow death of a vibration pump

Vibration pumps rarely fail with a bang. Mine took longer to build pressure than it used to, and then couldn't hold it through the shot, which is exactly the espresso machine pressure drop every forum thread describes before someone says "it's the pump." It was the pump.

The awkward part of a slow failure is that each shot is only slightly worse than the last. You adjust the grind, blame the beans, and re-tamp with more intent. It took longer than I'd like to admit before I stopped tuning around the problem and accepted that the hardware had changed underneath me.

## The like-for-like Lelit Anita pump replacement I didn't buy

The sensible fix is cheap and boring: order the stock Olab pump, swap it, done. I went to buy exactly that.

Then the optimizer in me asked the question it always asks. If the machine is going to be open on the counter anyway, what else is worth doing while I'm in there? That question has cost me money on every hobby I have. It has also produced most of the things I'm happiest with.

What I ordered instead:

- An **Olab Silent Green K09541L** (35W, 230V). Same family as the stock pump, built to be quiet.
- A **Quickmill vibration pump pulsor**, which sits on the pump outlet to smooth out the pulses a vibration pump produces.
- A **new OPV**. The over-pressure valve is what caps brew pressure, and on a five-year-old machine it deserved replacing rather than trusting.
- New tubes and fittings throughout, for longevity. If I'm replacing the heart, I'm not keeping five-year-old arteries.
- A **naked portafilter**, which I'd wanted for a while and finally had an excuse to buy.

## More plumbing than engineering

The swap itself is easy. It's more plumber work than anything: disconnect, reconnect, route the tubes, secure the pump. If you've kept your own equipment running for a while, this is a job, not a project.

The pulsor is the only real fit issue. It makes the pump assembly a bit too long for the space Lelit left for it. It fits — only just — and you notice it on the way in.

And then there's the part I'm less proud of. I couldn't find my Teflon tape, and I decided the joint between the brass pump outlet and the plastic pulsor would probably be fine without it. It was not fine. A couple of test runs with the lid still off showed a slow leak right at that joint. After a more rigorous search of the house, the tape turned up, the joint got wrapped properly, and the leak stopped. Test with the lid open. **Plumbing always wins the argument about shortcuts.**

## Quiet, steady, and better than before

I expected to get my old machine back. I got a better one.

The first thing you notice is the sound: the new pump and pulsor turn the brew into a low hum. The second thing takes a few shots to trust: pressure builds smoothly, holds steady, and the OPV caps it at its factory 9 bar. The extractions now look the same shot after shot, which is the whole point of a machine you've already dialed in.

What ends up in the cup changed too. Consistency is the big one: fewer shots I have to explain to myself. The crema is thicker and more luscious. And there's less of a temperature drop across the shot, so the last third of the extraction tastes like it belongs to the first.

## Finally, a naked portafilter

The bottomless portafilter was an upgrade I'd wanted for some time, and doing it at the same time as the pump was the right call. You can see everything: when the flow starts, whether it comes out evenly across the basket, and where it channels when your puck prep slips.

**That visibility is the real feature.** A spouted portafilter hides your mistakes and averages them into the cup. A naked one shows them to you in real time, which makes you better at avoiding them. The shots are cleaner too, with no spouts to collect old coffee and no second path for the espresso to pick up anything on the way down.

## The cost is every café espresso from now on

My home espresso was already better than what most coffee shops pour. Most of that comes from very good beans, a decent machine, and the kind of meticulous preparation a busy café can't afford. The upgrade moved it up a level.

That comes with a price I didn't budget for. Shop espresso was already a compromise I accepted when I was out. Now the gap is wider, and I notice it. I'll enjoy café coffee less from here on, and that's the honest downside of making something better at home: you can't un-taste the new baseline.

I've written before that [owning a machine is the real project](/voron-build-vs-maintenance/), and that [old things deserve to run properly](/astra-opc-diy-maintenance/) rather than just run. This was that lesson in its most rewarding form. Unlike [the smart drip machine I reviewed](/fellow-aiden-coffee-maker-review/), where the fun faded once it was dialed in, this one got better the moment I opened it up.

The needle doesn't sag anymore. It climbs, it holds, and the shot finishes with the same conviction it started with. Which is more than I can say for the next espresso I order in town.
